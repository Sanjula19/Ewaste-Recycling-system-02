How Component 4 Works, One Piece at a Time

Your terminal weighs a piece of waste, looks at its colour, asks a program on your laptop what to do with it, and then physically moves it to the right bin. This page builds that picture up one block at a time — starting from a single chip and ending with the whole system — so you can see where every wire and every message goes.

Before the diagrams: how to read them

Every diagram on this page uses the same four shapes and the same arrow rule. Learn these once and the rest follows.

Teal boxThe ESP32 — the small computer that runs your code.
Plain boxAn input — a sensor that measures something.
Copper boxAn output — something that moves, lights up, or beeps.
Grey boxSoftware — code running on your laptop, not on the bench.

Arrows show which way information travels. An arrow from a sensor into the ESP32 means the sensor is telling the ESP32 something. An arrow out of the ESP32 into a servo means the ESP32 is giving an order.

Building it up, step by step

Six steps. Each one adds a single new idea to the diagram before it.

01

Start with the brain

The ESP32 is a small chip on a board about the size of a stick of gum. It is a complete little computer: it runs a program you write (called firmware), it has metal pins along both edges that wires connect to, and it has built-in Wi-Fi.

Those pins are called GPIO pins — General Purpose Input/Output. "GPIO 21" just means "pin number 21". Every sensor and every motor in your build connects to specific numbered pins, and your code refers to them by those numbers.

02

Add the senses

Three sensors feed the ESP32. Each answers one question about the item on the plate.

  • TCS34725 — a colour sensor. It reports red, green and blue levels plus overall brightness. When something is placed on the plate it blocks the light, brightness drops, and that drop is how the terminal knows an item has arrived.
  • Load cell + HX711 — a scale. The load cell bends very slightly under weight; the HX711 is a small amplifier board that turns that tiny bend into a number the ESP32 can read.
  • HC-SR04 — an ultrasonic distance sensor. It chirps and times the echo, like a bat. Pointed down into the storage bin, a short distance means it is nearly full.
03

Send the readings to your laptop

The ESP32 is deliberately not smart. It does not know metal prices and cannot run a forecasting model. So it packs its readings into a short text message and sends it over Wi-Fi to the backend — the Python program already running on your laptop.

The message format is JSON: labelled values in curly braces. Sending it is an HTTP POST, the same kind of request your browser makes when you submit a form. The backend replies on the same connection with its decision.

Both devices must be on the same Wi-Fi network, and the backend must be started so it accepts connections from other devices rather than only from the laptop itself. That is the --host 0.0.0.0 setting.
04

The backend decides

This is your actual research contribution — the part that is not wiring. When a reading arrives, the backend sorts the item into one of two worlds and answers a different question for each.

05

Act on the answer

Now the ESP32 has an instruction, and the outputs do the physical work. A servo is a motor that turns to a specific angle and holds it — you tell it "go to 30 degrees" and it goes there, which is exactly what a sorting gate needs.

Those two servos pull far more electrical current than the ESP32 can supply. They need their own 5–6 V power adapter, with its ground wire joined to the ESP32's ground. Powering them from the ESP32 itself makes the board restart every time the crusher moves.
06

Watch the bin, and stop when it is full

One loop runs continuously in the background, separate from the sorting cycle. The ultrasonic sensor keeps measuring the distance down into the storage bin. When rubbish piles up past the safety line, the terminal refuses to accept anything more until the bin is emptied — the lockout.

The whole thing at once

The same six steps assembled into one picture. The dashed frames separate what sits on your bench from what runs on your laptop.

One item, start to finish

What actually happens, in order, when a single piece of scrap is dropped in.

  1. Something lands on the plateThe colour sensor sees brightness drop sharply. That is the trigger — nothing happens until it does.
  2. Wait half a secondThe scale is still wobbling. Reading it too early gives a wrong weight.
  3. MeasureTake several weight samples and average them; read red, green, blue and brightness.
  4. Ask the backendPack the numbers into JSON, POST them over Wi-Fi, beep once so the operator knows the scan registered.
  5. Backend answersMetal goes to the price forecast; plastic goes to the energy calculation. A short decision comes back.
  6. Show itGreen LED for SELL, yellow for HOLD.
  7. Move itThe gate servo swings to the matching bin. On HOLD, the crusher servo runs its compaction stroke first.
  8. Log itThe backend has already recorded the batch in the manifest, so it appears on the dashboard and in the end-of-cycle PDF.
  9. ResetWait for the plate to clear, then go back to waiting. Meanwhile the bin check keeps running in the background.

Words you will keep meeting

Firmware
The program that runs on the ESP32 itself. You write it in Arduino IDE and upload it over the USB cable.
GPIO
A numbered pin on the ESP32. Wires from sensors and motors connect to these, and your code names them by number.
I2C
A way for chips to talk over just two shared wires, SDA and SCL. Your colour sensor uses it.
PWM
Rapid on-off pulsing. Servos read the pulse length as the angle they should turn to.
Library
Ready-made code someone else wrote for a specific part — the ones you already installed.
JSON
A plain-text way of writing labelled values, used for the message the ESP32 sends.
HTTP POST
Sending data to a program over the network and getting a reply — the same mechanism a web form uses.
Backend
The FastAPI program on your laptop that holds all the intelligence.
Endpoint
One specific address the backend answers on, such as /api/iot/ingest.
Lockout
The state where the bin is full and the terminal refuses new items until it is emptied.
